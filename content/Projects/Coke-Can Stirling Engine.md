
Nearing the end of my second thermodynamics class during the second year of my engineering bachelor's, we were told that the assigned class final project would be to build a working Stirling engine; well, that and an accompanying written report explaining the physical principles that made the thing go. The class had a heavy emphasis on thermodynamic cycles so I guess it was more than appropriate and significantly easier than having us build a full on turbine.

Although not strictly the first real-world project I had worked on during my studies, I do have to say that this was one of the first times I felt my abilities and knowledge were falling short of what the situation demanded.

As one did back in the day, as soon as we were assigned the project, we jumped into YouTube to look for a decent tutorial on how to bring that nebulous thermodynamic cycle to the real world. There were no shortage of videos showcasing the wonders a guy with an angle grinder and some pieces of scrap wood could achieve, sadly my puny attempts at imitating their achievements only yielded a pile of very poorly cut wood, a compressed air can cut in half and a bunch of bent wires.

I eventually was lucky enough to find [this book](https://www.stirlingbuilder.com/quick-and-easy-stirling-engine) by Jim R. Larsen that did not only had the virtue of being a relatively simple γ-style design that could be built with the very rudimentary tools and abilities I had at hand, but also had detailed diagrams and instructions one could follow to get a working prototype.

Turns  out, more than any thermodynamic question of temperature differentials and flow paths, using an appropriate crankshaft geometry and reducing the friction between the engine displacer and the cylinder walls were the most critical parts of getting this engine to work.

I ended up getting a working engine the day before due date. Before leaving for class the next morning, I recorded this short video of the little engine working on top of my stove (please ignore my empty milk jugs) to show my teammate that, indeed, we had a working engine.

![](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=dRwNdhMW8us)

After a very frustrating week plagued with failed attempts, the feeling of finally seeing something you built working as it is supposed to is hard to beat. Although a relatively low stakes project and, to be honest, a relatively simple build, I still remember this little engine as one of the first things I built that made me feel like I could do more than just Legos.
